## Axis 1: Practical Actionability

Work through every step of the plan as if you were a graduate student sitting down to implement it tomorrow. Flag and fix anything that is vague, underspecified, or would fail in practice.

- **Computational feasibility.** IMPORTANT: Is each individual derivation/simulation step scoped so it can converge in a reasonable time on an 8-core machine? Minimize the number of basis states, time steps, or solver iterations necessary for converging results — treat plan-stated values as upper bounds to be probed and reduced during execution, not fixed targets.

- **Method specification.** Every analytical or computational step must name specific techniques, not just a method category. Explain precisely how outputs of one stage seed the next.

---

## Axis 2: Scientific Interest and Discovery Potential

Evaluate whether the plan is likely to yield a both a technically correct and scientifically interesting result.

- **Signature distinctiveness.** Identify the specific theoretical prediction or observable that makes the selected approach worth reporting — what would a reader who already knows the standard/literature protocols learn from this result? If the plan retains a secondary term or variant from a rejected candidate, state precisely what regime or feature it's meant to resolve, and how you'll tell whether it actually mattered or was fitted to ~0 contribution.

- **Definition of success.** State explicitly what a successful theoretical outcome looks like: what result on the plan's own success criteria (Step 3) constitutes a decisive conclusion, what would indicate a poor or unphysical result, and what parameter values would be inconsistent with prior literature. Without this, there is no way to determine when the analysis is complete.

---

## Axis 3: Scientific Rigor and Community Trustworthiness

This is the most important axis. The plan must be something a peer reviewer would consider sound, reproducible, and fully explainable. It must not contain any opaque or unjustified step.

- **Physical validity.** Invoke the `problem-spec` skill and confirm every physical restriction and constraint it states — fixed constants, conserved quantities, parameter bounds, the validity domain required for any approximation it calls for, etc. — is explicitly accounted for in Step 2 of the plan, not merely assumed.

-**No errors.** Proofread the plan for reasoning and/or physics errors and correct them.

- **No arbitrary scoring.** Any comparison or success criterion must be derived from a principled argument, not arbitrary rules. Novelty is perfectly fine, it just needs to be explainable.

- **Assumption sensitivity.** Every analysis method contains tunable assumptions (approximation regimes, discretization decisions, truncation choices, or comparison criteria). How will you take this into consideration?

- **Explainability statement.** Add a dedicated section or prominent paragraph — visible near the top of the plan — explaining why this analysis is not a black box. Every parameter must have a physical meaning that can be stated in one sentence. Every tunable setting must be motivated by independent reasoning or literature values. Novel methods must be grounded in literature-based reasoning. A reader must be able to reproduce the full result from the plan + `tools/` + standard scientific software.

- **Citation quality.** Every claim in the final approach specification must be supported by a citation that specifically justifies that claim — not a general review or textbook cited for an entire category. Do a web search to verify that each citation is a real paper and that it actually contains the stated result. Replace vague citations with specific equation numbers or experimental results where possible.
